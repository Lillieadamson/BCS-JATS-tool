Podcast as Policy Communicator

How Lived Experiences of Extreme Heat Events Can Inform Local Policy Conversations

Abstract

This project explores how sharing the lived experiences from the 2021 heat dome in the Capital Regional District (CRD) of British Columbia may serve as a tool for climate change communication. It examines how podcasting can be used as a communication tool of “policy listening.” This involves connecting priority populations and community service providers to share their lived experiences of the 2021 BC heat dome. It prioritizes listening to the stories of those disproportionately affected by the 2021 heat dome and enabling these stories to inform policy-making. This podcast accompanies a larger research project called A Hot Topic, as elaborated further in this issue. For our podcast, priority populations, or those disproportionately affected by extreme heat events (EHEs), include but are not limited to: socially isolated seniors, those with perinatal lived experience, such as pregnant or nursing people, Indigenous people, people who live in a rural location, those who take medication that may make them sensitive to extreme heat, people who are unhoused or precariously housed, and community service providers who may support any of these populations. We argue that to make more equitable policy outcomes and solutions, integration of the perspectives and voices of priority populations into policy development is essential. Podcasting is one platform to enhance policy conversations about extreme weather events so that policies do not inadvertently oppress those they are meant to serve. This research-informed podcast draws upon conversations with six participants from diverse priority populations, who shared their stories about extreme heat. To create more equitable policy outcomes, our findings indicate that policy- and decision-makers must tailor communication efforts to the needs and perspectives of members of priority populations, and there must be a sincere and honest effort to build community between those who are disproportionately affected, and those who have the capacity to provide support to these populations.

Additional episodes of A Hot Topic podcast can be found here: https://podcasts.apple.com/ca/podcast/a-hot-topic-addressing-heat-vulnerability-in/id1821551561

From 25 June to 1 July 2021, British Columbia experienced Canada’s deadliest extreme heat event (EHE), hereafter referred to as the heat dome, causing a record 619 deaths (BC Coroners Service 2022). An “extreme heat emergency” occurs “when daytime and nighttime temperatures get hotter every day and are well above seasonal norms” (Government of British Columbia 2026). While contributing to emergent climate justice scholarship that utilizes creative multimedia storytelling, such as podcasting, this project explored the creation and development of a publicly available podcast designed to amplify the voices of priority populations who disproportionately experienced adverse effects of the heat dome (Lew et al. 2024–25; Government of Canada 2022; Centre for Climate Justice 2024a, 2024b; Woolever 2022; Rosenblum and Gaertner 2024–25; Yoon et al. 2025a, 2025b). This study builds upon previous research in the Capital Regional District (CRD) to define priority populations, or those affected by EHEs, as including but not limited to: socially isolated seniors; those with perinatal lived experience, such as pregnant or nursing people; Indigenous people; people who live in a rural location; those who take medication that may make them sensitive to extreme heat; people who are unhoused or precariously housed; and community service providers who may support any of these populations (Wiebe and Mah 2023; Wiebe and Mah 2026, this issue). The previous research project, A Hot Topic: Addressing Heat Vulnerability to Extreme Heat Exposure in the Capital Regional District (CRD), was conducted by a research team from the University of Victoria and the CRD Community Health Network, and focused on gathering data on priority populations’ lived experiences of the heat dome (Wiebe and Mah 2023). To expand upon this existing research and to drive appropriate equity-informed intersectional policy creation (Hankivsky et al. 2019; Hoogeveen et al. 2021), A Hot Topic’s research team entered the next phase of the project by amplifying the voices of those most impacted by extreme heat through the creation and development of a podcast, A Hot Topic: Addressing Heat Vulnerability in the Capital Regional District. The purpose of the podcast is to explore how priority populations’ lived experiences can serve as a climate change communication tool with local policy- and decision-makers. We discuss the need to better integrate the perspectives and voices of priority populations into policy development through what we refer to as policy listening (see podcast episodes at Dolman et al. 2025–26; see also Wiebe and Mah 2026).

Methodology: Participatory Podcast Creation

Climate change inequitably affects individuals and communities across and within diverse populations. Until this project, current research had yet to explore how podcasting may serve as a tool for populations that are disproportionately affected by EHEs. In alignment with a community-based participatory research approach, whereby community partners are involved in every aspect of the research process (Hacker 2013, 2), this podcast sought to understand how podcasting can improve climate policy-makers’ understandings of the impact of their work by allowing them to hear directly from those most impacted by their decisions and recommendations.

While many people experienced adverse health impacts from the heat dome, this podcast specifically focused on amplifying the voices of those who face structural barriers that make them more vulnerable to the negative health impacts of extreme heat. These voices are often missed in the research that underlies climate action policy, and this study worked to amplify them to create more equitable climate action in the CRD. Within the academy, this study contributed to the growing discourse on the role of podcasting in academia and explored how the participatory action research (PAR) approach of lived experience as expertise may contribute to the creation of policies that prioritize the incorporation and amplification of these perspectives. This study combined academic podcasting, PAR, intersectionality-based policy analysis (IBPA), and phenomenology to examine and discuss how their interactions may contribute to new iterations of knowledge legitimization in academic and public policy spheres.

Intersectionality is defined as “the critical insight that race, class, gender, sexuality, ethnicity, nation, ability, and age operate not as unitary, mutually exclusive entities, but as reciprocally constructing phenomena that in turn shape complex social inequalities” (Collins 2015, 2). IBPA is “a policy tool to better illuminate how policy constructs individuals’ and groups’ relative power and privileges vis-à-vis their socio-economic-political status, health and well-being” (Hankivsky et al. 2019, 134). This lens served as a means to capture the “different dimensions of policy contexts including history, politics, everyday lived experiences, diverse knowledges and intersecting social locations,” which can then “generate transformative insights, knowledge, policy solutions and actions that cannot be gleaned from other equity-focused policy frameworks” (Hankivsky et al. 2019, 135). Further, IBPA drives equity for “systematically marginalized communities,” which “have typically contributed the least to climate change but are disproportionately negatively impacted by climate-related events, such as wildfires, extreme heat, flooding and extreme weather” (Hoogeveen et al. 2021, 6). This conceptual framework has an equity-informed approach that was applied to the design, production, and analysis of the interviews.

In practice, this study involved free-form interviews with people who identified with one or more of the identities of the proposed priority populations and resided in the CRD during the heat dome. The population studied was not representative of the general population but was diverse in age. The main question that this study answered is: How can the telling and sharing of priority populations’ lived experiences of the heat dome be used as a climate change communication tool? And the related subquestion included: How can podcasting be used as a communication tool for priority populations to share their lived experiences of the heat dome and contribute to policy listening?

Policy Listening Through Podcasting

Building from the Hot Topic project, a focus of the podcasting project was to encourage what we refer to as “policy listening” (see Wiebe and Mah 2026, this volume). Podcasting is lauded for its accessible nature. In a recent BC Studies special issue on relational technologies, Daisy Rosenblum and David Gaertner explain how podcasting can “redistribute expertise from the centre to the margins” (2024–25, 18) and mobilize knowledge to be in service of communities. They can also be useful for people with lower literacy levels, and those who work with their hands can listen while they work. Podcasts can serve as a powerful tool for accessible education (Lew et al. 2024–25). The UBC Centre for Climate Justice’s The Right to Be Cool project and podcast is an excellent example of this (Centre for Climate Justice 2024a; 2024b). Podcasting may reach a wider audience that may experience barriers in accessing information in other ways. In sum, Rosenblum and Gaertner put it well, noting that podcasting can serve as a site of “critical making, community-building, and knowledge-sharing beyond the extractive logics that currently fuel our digital environments” (2024–25, 20). This medium can enhance community care and well-being.

Recommendations

Based on the last four summers, extreme heat events are becoming the norm with increasing frequency. These events are time sensitive, urgent, and promptly require funding and resources, particularly in relation to preparation, specifically, of priority populations. Through this podcast, municipal, provincial, and federal policy-makers are invited to take action to respond to the needs of priority populations in their respective levels of government. As these conversations were conducted in the CRD, local government organizations are encouraged to develop comprehensive consultation processes that incorporate the perspectives of priority populations. The responses given by the interview participants in this podcast may serve as a resource and blueprint in the pursuit of expanding these efforts. Areas of focus include creating more resources tailored to the needs of renters, or those who are unhoused or precariously housed. For example, John Lamont, who lives in supported housing in the capital region, shares how most residents would not be able to afford an air conditioning unit, and his story reminds us how it continues to be unclear how to access rebates associated with easing the financial burden of purchasing one at full price (Dolman 2025b). Maggie Gay, a senior living alone, mentioned the challenges specific to living rurally, being reliant on unreliable public transportation, and not knowing where to look for support during extreme heat events (Dolman 2025d).

Another effort we recommend is for local governments to promote the use of tailored communication efforts that consider the unique needs of specific audiences. In particular, the needs of members of priority populations must be considered, which requires careful consideration and awareness of one’s neighbours. This could be paired with working with health authorities and local service providers to identify and support those most vulnerable to negative health outcomes of extreme heat. Ruth Waldick, an ecologist living on Salt Spring Island, discusses the Emergency Pod Program with the purpose of connecting people in the neighbourhood with important information, such as being aware that a senior is living in the area, or who has a generator, or which neighbour may have a farm and livestock. This creates an interconnected community whereby emergency response is embedded in the neighbourhood itself (Dolman 2025c). An Emergency Pod Program could be done through the development of outreach teams responsible for assessing and recording those who are members of priority populations that may need more support during EHEs.

As discussed by Eva Shaffer, one of the main priorities for her community of T’Sou-ke Nation is taking care of their Elders by ensuring they have clean water, are in air-conditioned spaces, and are not isolated. She emphasizes the need to engage in “real outreach” such as going “door-to-door mak[ing] sure people are doing okay, see[ing] what they’ve got, [and] what they need” (Dolman 2025a). A community leader echoed the significance of outreach as a key tool to support community members, citing as an example the Victoria Native Friendship Centre’s check-in program adapted from COVID-19 pandemic programming for Indigenous Elders to ensure they have everything they need to be comfortable during extreme heat events, though she notes that this is a very small population of people (personal communication, 4 July 2024).

An additional recommendation for local policy- and decision-makers could be the integration of podcasting practices that feature lived-experiences to increase outreach initiatives. Outreach was repeatedly mentioned by interview participants and is an integral part of moving forward in policy development that provides accessible care to those disproportionately affected by extreme heat events.

Concluding Reflections

[Everyone has] to be unified and organized and made to feel like there is something meaningful that they can do about [climate change] in a changing system whose outcome is unpredictable. —Local community organizer, 12 July 2024

The Hot Topic podcast emphasized the unique and individual nature of people’s lived experiences, and how hearing and prioritizing these stories is the way to advocate for the needs of priority populations. By developing these interviews into a publicly available podcast, we contribute to the emerging practice of using podcasting to share information with local policy- and decision-makers, as elaborated in our concluding episode (Lew et al. 2024–25; Government of Canada 2022; Centre for Climate Justice 2024a, 2024b; Woolever 2022; Rosenblum and Gaertner 2024–25; Yoon et al. 2025a, 2025b). In this way, our podcast makes key contributions to the fields of public administration and policy communication specifically, and academia more generally.

Speaking directly with members of priority populations also aligns with intersectionality-based policy analysis (IBPA), participatory action research (PAR), and phenomenological approaches as it provides an example of the legitimization of their lived experiences as actual and reputable knowledge. This connected, caring, and intimate work is an integral part of building trusting relationships and connections with members of priority populations while contributing to emergent scholarship in the study and practice of relational technologies, which takes time to cultivate (Rosenblum and Gaertner 2024–25). This is a pillar of IBPA, PAR, and phenomenologically informed interpretive analysis. Such a relational approach underscores the importance of adapting to building relationships in a way that meets community members where they are at. This means physically, mentally, and emotionally meeting them where they feel comfortable and at ease and tailoring the consultative process to their individual needs. This project, with a PAR approach, sought to integrate the interviewees in a process that prioritized their lived experience. Questions were designed to elicit responses from a subjective, personal, and first-hand engagement of the event, as opposed to an objective, detached perspective. Further, the questions were designed to be more general, rather than specific, and not leading, to allow a variety of interpretations and responses that provided space for interviewees to safely and openly express themselves and share their subjective experience. Based on the interviewees’ responses, and the diversity of each person’s answers to the same question, the individual’s storied lived and living experiences resonate. Lived experience can not only be perceived as data, in the context of academia, but as a way to subvert the stereotypical framework of objective research approaches that may perceive subjective and interpretive stories as illegitimate sources of data. Stories are indeed carriers of knowledge and wisdom.

Some key lessons for policy-makers and those listening to the podcast include that building relationships and respecting community expertise are integral pieces of the puzzle in developing equitable policy outcomes. The methods used to conduct this study (i.e., meeting people where they were at in a setting of their choosing, with open questioning) offer a window into the way a trusting foundation can be formed between researcher and interviewee and between one community member, the researcher, to another, the interviewee. Creating tailored communication efforts that are designed for members of priority populations themselves is also an area that could be further expanded upon in future studies.

Not everyone is afforded the resources to cope with climate change evenly. There must be unification and organization, as the local community organizer cited above reminds us, and connection, to prepare for, cope with, and deal with the aftermath of extreme heat events across the CRD. An equitable, inclusive, consultative process built on relationship building and community awareness is integral to providing an equitable environment in which we can collectively cope with the effects of climate change, together.

Contributor Bios

Erica Dolman is the program coordinator for the Coastal Climate Solutions Leaders (CCSL) program at the University of Victoria. She holds a master of public administration from the University of Victoria and a bachelor of arts in political science from the University of British Columbia. Her research interests include climate change communication, public policy, knowledge mobilization, and public engagement. Her master’s research examined the use of podcasting as a tool to amplify the lived experiences of populations disproportionately affected by extreme heat and to foster more inclusive climate policy conversations. Through her work at CCSL, Erica supports interdisciplinary graduate training and collaboration to prepare the next generation of climate leaders.

Kirsten Mah grew up on Treaty 6 and Treaty 7 territory in Red Deer, Alberta, under the shadow of the Northern Alberta oil sands. She learned first-hand the tension that arises for many residents between their understanding and commitment to planetary health and their economic realities. She works for the Capital Regional District as a community health planner. In this role she supports the region’s Community Health Network, which strives to create healthy, holistic communities through research and partnership. She holds a master of public and international affairs from the University of Ottawa. She has worked in social and health sectors across Canada and internationally where she has learned the importance of equitable approaches to research and service delivery. 

Sarah Marie Wiebe is an associate professor in the School of Public Administration at the University of Victoria. Her research expertise includes democratic engagement and deliberative policy analysis, climate and environmental justice, and community-engaged research. She is a co-founder of FERN (Feminist Environmental Research Network) and author of several books, including most recently Hot Mess: Mothering Through a Code Red Climate Emergency.
